The Woozle Effect

George Hovey

The opinions of the author do not necessarily reflect those of the Department of War, U.S. Strategic Command, or any other entity.

Welcome to the Hundred Acre Wood.

You may think that in Winnie-the-Pooh, A.A. Milne was writing about stuffed animals and childhood fantasy. The truth of it is, Milne was a visionary satirist, writing about emerging technology and geopolitics in the 21st century. Which is likely why George Orwell based the concept of Animal Farm on the Hundred Acre Wood. Despite popular sentiment, Milne was not referring to homely authoritarian rulers. Pooh and friends are allegorical figures representing entire swaths of the AI ecosystem. Uncovered documents show that in the author’s original conception, Pooh depicts the frontier labs. He has all the honey he wants yet always desires more, and while he suffers setbacks, usually of his own making, he always bounces back and carries gleefully on. Rabbit is the protectionist regulator whose plans invert: his scheme to drive Kanga from the forest makes her a permanent resident, while Piglet, a small ally that always gets caught in the crossfire, takes the collateral damage. Tigger is the techno-optimist who bounces up trees he cannot climb down. Owl is the pundit who uses words he cannot comprehend and is consulted anyway. Poohsticks, a game of arbitrary luck, is the benchmark leaderboard that gets all the coverage.

Pooh circles the thicket hunting woozles, growing more certain of the threat with every new set of prints, until he discovers the tracks are his own. The Woozle effect works the same way. A questionable claim is made without supporting evidence, later reports cite the original without context, and the citations accumulate until the claim reads as established fact.

3. This and everything that follows is, to the best of my knowledge, true. The Woozle effect, named after this story in Winnie-the-Pooh, was coined by criminologist Beverly D. Houghton in November 1979 and popularized by Richard Gelles. The Woozle effect would, for example, explain why everyone “knows” that you need to drink eight glasses of water a day. A 1945 U.S. Food and Nutrition Board recommending 2.5 liters daily noted that most of that quantity is already contained in prepared foods. It was misquoted without this stipulation, and the misquotes were re-quoted until the eight-glass figure became common knowledge. (Heinz Valtin, American Journal of Physiology).

What does this have to do with the price of compooht in China? A lot, as it would turn out.

The Woozle race

The debate surrounding the AI race has several woozles, the most consequential of which is the race framing itself.

The AI race is usually framed as the market-oriented West competing against the Chinese Communist Party’s (CCP) centrally orchestrated blueprint for AI dominance.iEndnote i Eric Schmidt, who chaired the National Security Commission on Artificial Intelligence, articulated the binary plainly: the American system is “relatively decentralized” and “typically not coherently planned,” whereas “the Chinese model is centrally planned. The goals are set out.”iiEndnote ii No evidence is expected, because the claim has been repeated long enough to feel like background knowledge. The binary fails in the other direction as well: the CHIPS Act, the AI Action Plan, and export controls were all designed to shape the free market America claims not to plan.

The U.S.-China Economic and Security Review Commission’s 2025 Annual Report likewise framed China’s outcomes as engineered by design, describing a “whole-of-nation effort” of state-led industrial policy that generates “interlocking innovation flywheels,” with Military-Civil Fusion (MCF) harnessing commercial innovation for the military.iiiEndnote iii But the analysts most cited on MCF also caution the Beltway against distorting it. Elsa Kania and Lorand Laskai, who did the defining research, note that a 2020 presidential proclamation defined MCF as the acquisition and diversion of foreign technology, a definition they call largely divorced from the crux of the PRC policies under the MCF umbrella. Kania and Laskai warn that portrayals of MCF unmoored from the underlying policy risk flawed diagnosis and misaligned responses.ivEndnote iv

The specialist literature also diverges from the state-engineered framing. RAND’s 2025 study of Chinese AI industrial policy concludes that China’s progress “is likely to continue to be driven by its innovative private tech firms and start-ups,” and that the lack of consensus about where AI is going makes long-term state planning hard to execute.vEndnote v Barry Naughton, who coined “grand steerage” to describe Xi-era economic ambition, rejects the word “planning” to describe Beijing’s approach: it “suggests outdated instruments,” while China’s economy is “now predominantly market-based.”viEndnote vi David Lin, a senior advisor at the Special Competitive Studies Project (which Schmidt founded) puts it sharply: Beijing is “far more adept at harvesting the results of involution, even as it struggles to govern the process.”viiEndnote vii

Chinese provinces’ approach implicitly rejects the premise of centralized coordination. Anhui’s own development plan names regional competition with other provinces, not national security or Beijing’s directives, as the reason for its AI plan.viiiEndnote viii Xi Jinping has publicly complained about the resulting involution (over-competition leading to waste): “When it comes to new projects, it’s always the same few things: artificial intelligence, computing power, and new energy vehicles. Do all provinces in the country have to develop industries in these areas?”ixEndnote ix That’s less of a blueprint than a bear looking at his map, wondering where the tracks came from.

There is not a Grand Pooh-Bah in Beijing, pulling the CCP puppet strings, directing every company like a piece on a Claymation set. Beijing probably did not make TikTok a global sensation so it could harvest the metadata of Americans doing silly dances and hand it to Unitree to train the next generation of kung fu robots. Unitree built robots simply “because the orders came in.”xEndnote x China’s AI ecosystem faces the same market dynamics Western ones do, and the sooner we accept that, the sooner we can write policy that responds to the real China instead of the cartoon version of one.

DeepSeek proliferates woozles

In January 2025 the non-AI world was introduced to DeepSeek. Two “facts” arrived with it: DeepSeek had trained a frontier-class model for $5.576 million, and it had smuggled 50,000 Nvidia H100 GPUs to do so. Both claims were repeated by serious people, and both diverge from their sources. They pull in opposite directions: if DeepSeek built a frontier model on a shoestring, why would it need two billion dollars of contraband? Each claim was deployed for a different argument: the cheap model to show export controls had failed and the hidden GPUs to show they had been evaded, but both came from following woozles instead of the evidence.

The $5.576 million cost figure is in the V3 technical report: 2.8 million H800 GPU-hours at an assumed two dollars per rental hour.xiEndnote xi The same paragraph states that “the aforementioned costs include only the official training of DeepSeek-V3, excluding the costs associated with prior research and ablation experiments on architectures, algorithms, or data.” The figure describes one complete training round, nowhere near the capital required to build the system that made it possible. SemiAnalysis later put DeepSeek’s total server capital expenditure near $1.6 billion.xiiEndnote xii DeepSeek published the caveat, but the public’s attention stopped at the headline. On 27 January, Nvidia lost roughly $589 billion of market value, the largest one-day loss for any company in U.S. stock market history.xiiiEndnote xiii

The claim of 50,000 H100 GPUs came from a single sentence on CNBC. Scale AI’s Alexandr Wang, asked about DeepSeek on 23 January, claimed: “My understanding is that DeepSeek has about 50,000 H-100s, which they can’t talk about, obviously, because it is against the export controls.”xivEndnote xiv Elon Musk replied “Obviously” to a clip of the assertion posted on X.xvEndnote xv On the strength of that rumor, the number entered circulation as an established fact about sanctions evasion. The best available evidence comes from SemiAnalysis, which reports that DeepSeek has a fleet of roughly 50,000 Hopper GPUs, a family that includes the H800 and H20, both legal for export to China at the relevant times. That is a very different claim from 50,000 H100s, although the same reporting indicates that they may have acquired up to 10,000 H100s.xviEndnote xvi With the flip of a word, an impressive technical feat turned into a historic smuggling operation.

I suspect, without evidence, that these map onto distinct but related cognitive mechanisms. People who were already doubtful that export controls work seized on evidence that indicated the controls failed or backfired: asymmetric scrutiny or confirmation bias. People holding a prior belief that China cannot innovate at the frontier, or who over-index on compute, reached for an explanation that let them keep it: cognitive dissonance reduction. It is an unfalsifiable conjecture, but consistent with human psychology.

Another DeepSeek example: Feroot Security discovered code in DeepSeek’s web login page that could send some user login information to China Mobile. Feroot shared its findings with The Associated Press and the AP verified the finding with two academics and then published an article under the title “Researchers link DeepSeek’s blockbuster chatbot to Chinese telecom banned from doing business in US.”xviiEndnote xvii Feroot published its own account the same day under the title “Feroot Security Research Reveals DeepSeek AI’s Hidden Data Pipeline to China,”xviiiEndnote xviii describing “hidden capabilities enabling direct data transmission” to China Mobile and quoting its CEO’s remarks to ABC News: “We see direct links to servers and companies in China that are under control of the Chinese government. This is something we’ve never seen before.” Yet the AP reported that neither Feroot nor the other researchers observed any data being transferred to China Mobile when testing logins in North America. The code Feroot described appeared to collect details about the user’s device, a practice called fingerprinting that tech companies around the world use for security, verification, and ad targeting – a far cry from a direct pipeline to the CCP. The Independent’s rewrite ran under the headline “DeepSeek code may send U.S. user data straight to the Chinese government.”xixEndnote xix Each iteration exaggerates and bends the original claim, most egregiously in the headlines. The message resonated: The Select Committee on the Strategic Competition Between the United States and the Chinese Communist Party published a report on DeepSeek whose first key finding was: “DeepSeek funnels Americans’ data to the PRC through backend infrastructure connected to a U.S. government-designated Chinese military company.”xxEndnote xx At a later House hearing, ranking member Krishnamoorthi stated: “DeepSeek is sending our data straight into the hands of the CCP.”xxiEndnote xxi

The social credit system

An earlier woozle infestation plagued reporting on China’s social credit system. The story that consolidated in Western coverage was a techno-dystopian national system assigning every Chinese citizen a single score, updated in real time by algorithms, governing their access to travel, credit, and employment.xxiiEndnote xxii Then–Vice President Mike Pence described it as “an Orwellian system premised on controlling virtually every facet of human life.”xxiiiEndnote xxiii

The coverage did not get this from the scholars who read the source documents. Rogier Creemers concluded it was more accurate to think of the system as an ecosystem of loosely related initiatives sharing a rough logic than as a unified machine for social control.xxivEndnote xxiv Others documented that the citizen-score framing was wildly off base, and what actually existed was a patchwork of local pilots, separate corporate loyalty schemes, and blacklists — much of it administered on paper.xxvEndnote xxv

The dystopian version was too good a story to go away, and it confirmed what the audience already believed about Chinese technology. It certainly didn’t hurt that the tracks were real. There were blacklists, there was surveillance, and there were restrictions on travel for people who lost court cases. And the human rights abuses of the CCP’s surveillance state, including AI systems built to track ethnic minorities, are well documented.xxviEndnote xxvi Nonetheless, the misperceptions around the social credit system fit a pattern of techno-orientalism, projecting onto Chinese systems a degree of technological control and social engineering that the systems do not possess. When you are busy gaping at a grand conspiracy, it is hard to see the prints that led you to it.

Woozles of the world

America does not have a monopoly on woozles. A PLA Daily article, “算法战：牵引美军人工智能军事化应用” (Algorithmic Warfare: Driving the U.S. Military’s Militarization of AI) in November 2017,xxviiEndnote xxvii reported remarks by Lt. Gen. Jack Shanahan, who led the then-nascent Project Maven, on the status of his team’s work. Maven at that point was a narrow $70 million object-recognition pilot program for drone video, with four algorithms ready for testing. The purpose of the algorithms was workload relief: performing basic tasks like sorting, labeling, and describing. The PLA Daily piece distorted its source. It said the algorithms were “expected to be put into actual combat application soon” (预计很快将投入实战应用), while the Defense One source it drew on only said it would be “delivered by the end of the year” to a program office – presumably for testing, not combat employment.xxviiiEndnote xxviii

The text was mirror-published the same day across Xinhua, China News Service, and Sina, and was later cited as authority in peer-reviewed scholarship.xxixEndnote xxix A 2020 analytical piece stated that “according to Defense Systems, the AWCFT had developed four intelligent algorithms by the end of 2019, expected to enter combat application soon.”xxxEndnote xxx The embellished phrase survived verbatim because the copy was being copied, while the date was transposed by two years. Maven did eventually grow into something that would justify the PLA’s concern.xxxiEndnote xxxi But the claim, based on a single trade-press item, far outpaced the capability. Four algorithms in testing engendered a doctrine invented to explain them. Sensationalist headlines become doctrinal claims, which become academic citations. That is a Wizzle.

Woozles in the weights

This tale also has a recursive layer: AI entering citation chains. As new models are trained on unstructured corpora and synthetic data that is increasingly sourced from LLM-generated outputs, woozles of their own making will nest in the model weights. As AI becomes more thoroughly ingrained in our most critical systems, ensuring access to the ground truth becomes increasingly important. That is a technical problem, and people are working on it. It is also a governance question that demands an answer: what is our tolerance for delusion, and what standard of evidence do we intend to hold ourselves to?

A closing caution: this is a world where a foolish bear can chase woozles and still get his honey… and Piglet’s too. It can be easy to slip into lax standards. As Winnie-the-Pooh never said: “Woozle me once, shame on you. Woozle me twice, and it shall pass peer review.”
